It's an easy trade to reach for: move a bit further out, pay less for the same size of home, and put the difference towards something else. The part that's easy to underweight is what the longer journey actually costs, in money and in time you don't get back.

## The direct cost is bigger than a season ticket line item

A daily commute has a fare, but it usually also has knock-on costs that don't show up until you're already living somewhere: a longer walk or a bus leg at each end, more frequent takeaway meals or coffees on longer days, and sometimes childcare pickup windows that are harder to hit. None of these are dramatic individually, but stacked over a year they can meaningfully close the gap that made the further-out home look cheaper in the first place.

## Time is the cost that's easy to ignore

An extra 20 minutes each way is nearly 3 hours a week, or roughly 150 hours a year - the equivalent of almost four working weeks. That's before accounting for how a longer, more crowded journey tends to be more tiring than a shorter one, which has a real effect on evenings and weekends even if it never appears on a spreadsheet.

## Put real numbers against it before deciding

Rather than guessing, it's worth checking the actual journey - not the distance on a map, but what public transport genuinely takes at the time you'd really travel. ## A way to think about the trade-off

A useful frame is to ask what the time and money difference is actually worth to you, specifically - not in the abstract, but for the life you'd be living in that home. Saving £200 a month on rent for an extra 25 minutes each way is a genuinely different decision for someone who works from home three days a week than for someone commuting five days with an early school drop-off either side of it. There's no universally right answer, only one that accounts honestly for both sides of the trade.
