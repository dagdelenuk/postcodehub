Council Tax is one of the more predictable costs of living somewhere, but it's also one of the more commonly misunderstood - largely because the band a property sits in has nothing to do with what it's worth today.

## Bands are frozen in 1991

Every home in England was assigned a Council Tax band (A to H) based on what it would have sold for on 1 April 1991 - not its current value. A flat that's since been extended, or a street that's since become far more sought-after, generally keeps its original band regardless of how much prices have moved since. This is why band and current market value can drift apart significantly over three decades, and why it's worth checking a specific property's actual band rather than assuming from its current price.

## The same band costs different amounts in different places

Each council sets its own Band D rate every year, and every other band is a fixed proportion of that figure (Band A is 6/9ths of Band D, Band H is twice Band D, and so on). That means a Band D home in one London borough can cost meaningfully more or less per year than a Band D home in a neighbouring one - the band tells you the relative size of the bill within a borough, not the absolute amount until you know that borough's own rate. PostcodeHub's [Statistics pages](/london/) show every borough's current Council Tax by band, set directly against the London average, so it's a straightforward comparison rather than needing to look each one up separately.

## It's a genuine, recurring cost worth budgeting properly

Unlike many one-off costs of moving, Council Tax is an ongoing monthly bill for as long as you live somewhere. When comparing two homes at a similar price in different areas, it's worth adding the Council Tax difference into the real cost of living there, the same way you would with a longer commute - a lower purchase price or rent in one area can be partly or fully offset by a higher band, or a higher rate in that borough, once you look at it over a full year rather than as a one-off number.

## Checking before you commit

A property's actual band (not an estimate) is public record and worth confirming directly rather than assuming from similar homes nearby, especially for anything that's been extended or renovated since 1991. Once you know the band, comparing the actual annual cost across the areas you're considering - using each borough's current rate - gives a fair, recurring-cost comparison to sit alongside the purchase price or rent itself.
