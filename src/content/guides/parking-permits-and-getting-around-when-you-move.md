Parking is one of the more locally variable things on a moving checklist - the rules, the cost, and even what counts as a "resident" can differ meaningfully from one council to the next, sometimes street by street within the same borough.

## Controlled parking zones

Most residential streets in inner and much of outer London sit inside a controlled parking zone (CPZ), meaning parking is restricted to permit holders (or pay-and-display) during set hours - often, but not always, weekdays only. It's worth checking whether your specific street is inside a zone before assuming you can just park outside your new home, since a CPZ boundary can end mid-street with no obvious visual difference from outside it.

## Permit cost and eligibility vary a lot

Unlike Council Tax, which follows a fairly standard band structure everywhere, resident parking permit schemes are set entirely by each council - cost, how many permits a household can hold, and how they're priced (a flat fee, or scaled by the vehicle's emissions or engine size) all differ. Many councils now charge more for larger or higher-emission vehicles, and some cap the number of permits per household regardless of how many residents there are, so it's worth checking your specific council's scheme rather than assuming it matches what you're used to.

## Visitor permits

Most schemes also offer visitor permits, sold in books or as scratch-cards, for anyone coming to see you who needs to park in the zone. These usually need to be applied for separately from your own resident permit and are worth sorting out before you actually need one, since some councils only issue them online with a short processing time.

## Blue Badge

The Blue Badge disabled parking scheme is a national scheme, but it's applied for and administered through your local council, and a badge from your previous local authority is valid across the whole country - you don't need to reapply purely because you've moved, only when it's due for renewal.

## Dropped kerbs

If you have or want off-street parking, you generally need council permission for a dropped kerb (lowering the pavement to allow vehicle access), even on a property you own outright - this is a planning matter, not just a driveway one, and doing it without permission can result in being asked to reinstate the kerb at your own cost.

## Getting around without a car

If you're deciding whether you need a car at all in a new area, it's worth looking at realistic journey times by public transport first - PostcodeHub's [commute checker](/commute/) compares journey times from an area to wherever you actually need to get to, which is often a more useful starting point than parking cost alone.
