If you've read a few school admissions policies, you've probably seen "distance" appear somewhere in the priority order — usually well below siblings, looked-after children, and children with an education, health and care plan, but often the deciding factor once those higher categories are full.

## What "distance" usually means

Most London councils measure the straight-line ("as the crow flies") distance between a child's home and the school, not the walking or driving route. That has a real consequence: a house 600 metres from a school by road can be measured as much closer than that in a straight line if the school sits across a park, a railway line, or a river. Some authorities use a shortest-safe-walking-route measurement instead, so the exact method genuinely varies by council — always check the specific school's admissions policy rather than assuming.

## Why last year's cut-off distance isn't a promise

Oversubscribed schools publish the distance of the last child admitted the previous year, and it's tempting to treat that as a threshold. It isn't one. That number moves every year with how many families apply, how many siblings are already at the school, and how many other priority categories are filled first. A cut-off of 0.4 miles one year can become 0.6 miles the next simply because more families with younger siblings applied.

## Where a general area view actually helps

None of this means location is irrelevant — it's usually the single biggest lever a family has. What's worth doing early is narrowing down which part of a borough is even worth researching school-by-school, using things that don't change year to year: which postcode districts are genuinely near several schools, what the wider area is like to live in, and how it compares to alternatives you're also considering. PostcodeHub's [borough pages](/london/) list every school in a district by postcode area with its current Ofsted rating, and [Compare areas](/compare/) lets you put two or three districts side by side on the same page.

## What to actually verify before you commit to anything

- The specific school's own admissions policy and how it defines distance
- Last year's cut-off distance, treated as a rough guide only, not a guarantee
- Whether the address falls inside any separately defined catchment or priority area
- The application deadline for the intake year you need

A shortlist built from good local information is a sensible starting point. The admissions decision itself always needs the school and the local authority to confirm the details directly.
